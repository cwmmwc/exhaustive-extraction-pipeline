# Kimi K2.5 and the Extraction Pipeline

**Christian McMillen, Department of History, University of Virginia — March 2026**

---

The central computational challenge of my research is extracting structured information from nearly 5,000 historical PDFs — 139 million words of letters, hearing transcripts, court records, and field reports documenting federal Native American land dispossession. The extraction pipeline I have built sends each document to a large language model, which reads the text and returns structured records: every person, fee patent, financial transaction, piece of testimony, tax record, and relationship it finds. The question has been which AI model to use.

Anthropic's Claude is the most capable model for this work. It extracts the most detailed records and produces the strongest analytical synthesis when used as the reasoning model. But Claude is a commercial product that charges per use, and running the full corpus would cost several thousand dollars — creating a dependency that limits the transferability of the tools, particularly to tribal nations. I therefore tested every major open-source alternative: Meta's Llama 3.3 70B, Llama 4 Maverick, Llama 4 Scout, Alibaba's Qwen 2.5 72B, and Moonshot AI's Kimi K2.5.

The results were decisive. Meta's models extracted 25–46% of Claude's output and missed most of the individual narratives that make these documents historically valuable. Fine-tuning Llama on Claude's output made things worse. Qwen 2.5 72B, tested on the UVA high-performance computing cluster, produced 54% of Claude's total items but found only 26 fee patents where Claude found 169 and Kimi found 268. Fee patents — the records of individual land transfers from trust to private ownership — are the atomic unit of dispossession in this research. A model that cannot find them cannot do the work.

Kimi K2.5 changed the picture entirely. On the single most important benchmark document — a 221-page compilation of Board of Indian Commissioners field reports covering dozens of reservations — Kimi identified 268 unique fee patent allottees to Claude's 169. Those are 99 additional named individuals whose dispossession is documented in the historical record that Claude did not extract. Kimi also correctly distinguished between different legal mechanisms of dispossession (fee patents by application versus certificates of competency under the Kaw Treaty), a distinction Claude labeled generically. On the full Survey of Conditions corpus — 26 of 48 volumes of Senate hearings spanning 1927–1943 — Kimi extracted 158,351 structured records, including 2,487 fee patents, 5,492 testimony records, 1,880 tax records, and 703 mortgages.

Why Kimi succeeds where other open-source models fail is not fully understood. Kimi is a Mixture of Experts model with 1 trillion total parameters but only 32 billion active on any given token. However, Llama 4 Maverick — also a Mixture of Experts model — fails at the same task, so architecture alone does not explain Kimi's advantage. What is clear empirically is that the ability to read a compressed biographical narrative ("Harry Stubbs — land sold. Funds gone. Living off his friends.") and recognize it as a structured case of land dispossession is something Kimi learned during training that neither Llama nor Qwen acquired, regardless of parameter count. On the same 40,000-character chunk, Llama found 8 fee patents, Qwen found 2, and Kimi found 87 (Claude found 83).

Where Kimi falls short: it captures less per-record detail than Claude (sparser fields for acreage, sale price, and buyer), and it is weaker on legislative correspondence and long causal chains (58–66% of Claude on narrative-heavy documents). When tested as an analysis model on the same Kimi-extracted data from the 221-page benchmark document, Claude Opus produced deeper analytical framing — naming structural concepts, connecting to historiography, and building multi-stage arguments — while Kimi's analysis was competent but more descriptive. Testing three of four combinations of {Claude, Kimi} extraction × {Claude, Kimi} analysis on that document pointed toward **Kimi extraction → Claude Opus analysis** as the optimal pipeline: the widest evidence base interpreted with the deepest analytical framing. This has not yet been tested on the Survey of Conditions corpus.

Kimi K2.5 requires 549GB of model weights, which means it needs 8x A100 80GB GPUs just to load into memory. We run it three ways: via Together AI's hosted API ($0.50/$2.80 per million tokens — convenient but unreliable at scale), on UVA's HPC cluster via the LawData allocation (8x A100, no per-token cost), and through UVA Research Computing's new RC GenAI service, which launched on March 30, 2026 running Kimi K2.5 on eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs — available free to UVA researchers via API. The optimal pipeline is now clear: Kimi for extraction, Claude Opus for analytical synthesis, and university-owned infrastructure for independence and reliability.
