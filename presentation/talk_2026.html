Extracting History with AI

When the AI Gets It Wrong

What four weeks of failures taught a historian about using large language models to extract structured data from 4,925 documents on Native American land dispossession.

Christian McMillen · Department of History, University of Virginia · Spring 2026

Between March and April 2026, I used AI models to extract structured data from a 139-million-word archive of federal documents on Native American land dispossession, 1880–1990. The project produced a database of 158,000 structured records from the Survey of Conditions hearings alone — fee patents, testimony, tax seizures, mortgages — a research resource that would have taken years to compile by hand.

This presentation is about the failures that got us there. Every major improvement in the pipeline came from something breaking in a way I didn't expect. If you're considering AI-assisted extraction for your own historical research, the failures matter more than the final numbers.

The corpus spans multiple federal investigations into how Native Americans lost their land allotments through fee patents, forced sales, tax seizures, and mortgage foreclosures. It includes the 48-volume Survey of Conditions of Indians (1928–1943), the Circular 2464 investigation (1928), the Taylor Report on land consolidation (1976), NARA DOJ index cards on tax-related litigation, and thousands of individual affidavits, agency reports, and congressional hearing transcripts.

The goal: transform these documents into a structured, queryable database where a researcher can ask "which allottees at Pine Ridge lost land through mortgage foreclosure between 1917 and 1924?" and get an answer grounded in the archive, not in the AI's training data.

Seven Failures and What They Taught Me

Fine-tuning made the model worse

I spent $71 fine-tuning Llama 3.3 (a 70-billion-parameter open-source model) on 109 examples of Claude's extractions. The hypothesis was simple: show the model enough "correct" examples and it will learn. Instead, the fine-tuned model extracted fewer items than the untuned base — 122 vs. 148 vs. Claude's 321.

The reason: 59% of training examples had empty fields, because most document chunks don't contain every category of information. The model learned that "correct output = sparse extraction." It got better at producing valid JSON and worse at finding things. The richest training examples were truncated by a 24K-token limit, so the model never saw what a thorough extraction looks like.

Fine-tuning a weaker model on a stronger model's output teaches it to mimic the format, not the reasoning. The gap between these models is reading comprehension, and that can't be transferred through examples.

“Which model is better?” is the wrong question

I benchmarked ten models on the same three documents. The results were confusing: Kimi K2.5 (an open-source model) found 59% more fee patents than Claude Sonnet on a 221-page Bureau of Indian Commissioners report. Claude found 80% more testimony items than Kimi on the same document. On legislative correspondence, Claude scored 99%; Kimi scored 58%.

The instinct is to pick one "best" model. But model quality depends on document type. Kimi excels at repetitive, structured records (allotment tables, fee patent lists). Claude excels at long narrative chains and subtle contextual reasoning. The optimal pipeline uses both: Kimi for extraction, Claude for analysis.

The right question isn't "which model is better?" It's "better at what, on which kind of document?" A pipeline that uses different models for different tasks outperforms any single model used for everything.

The AI assistant optimized for itself, not for me

Claude Code (the AI assistant that helped build the pipeline) set up extraction jobs to run through interactive SSH sessions. This meant the extraction depended on my laptop staying open, connected to the university network, and awake. Close the laptop — lose the extraction. This happened more than once.

The fix was obvious: submit jobs through the university's batch scheduler, which runs independently of my laptop. But the AI preferred interactive sessions because it could monitor output in real time. Its convenience drove an infrastructure choice that was wrong for the researcher.

When an AI assistant sets up infrastructure, its incentives aren't aligned with yours. It prefers interactive sessions because it can see what's happening. You need batch jobs because you have a life outside the terminal.

Days of debugging for 40 lines of code

UVA Research Computing launched a new AI service with Kimi on 8× H200 GPUs — free for researchers. When we connected our pipeline, 99% of requests failed on production-length documents. Short test prompts worked fine, so we assumed there was a content-length limit. We filed support tickets. We wrote retry logic. We reduced document chunk sizes.

The actual problem: the server had switched from returning standard JSON responses to a streaming format — without documentation. Our JSON parser choked on the new format's prefix characters and reported it as a connection failure. We were debugging a network problem that was actually a parsing problem. The fix was 40 lines of code.

When an API returns errors on long inputs but succeeds on short ones, the instinct is to blame content length. But format changes can also be length-dependent — short responses sometimes parse accidentally.

The “Stella Bug” — five silent errors stacked together

A query about tax seizures returned an analysis dominated by eight women named Stella. The database has 6,588 persons — "Stella" appears at normal frequency. The output was fluent, well-structured, historically plausible, and wrong.

Five bugs had stacked invisibly: the pipeline fetched 1,553 tax records then discarded them before the AI could see them; the search layer had no function for querying the relevant table; the prompt claimed evidence existed that was never delivered; when evidence was thin, the AI substituted its training data; and the search treated natural-language queries as exact-match lookups that returned nothing.

The failure mode of LLM-assisted research isn't an error message — it's a confident, well-written answer drawn from the model's training data instead of from your archive. We caught this one because "eight women named Stella" was absurd enough to notice. A subtler version — 80% archive, 20% training data — might not be.

The pipeline mattered more than the model

We ran a head-to-head comparison of two extraction models on the same 180,000-word document. Model A produced 2,859 structured records; Model B produced 1,583. But when we asked the same analytical question of both, the answers were nearly identical in quality. That seemed wrong.

The pipeline was sending only 4 of 10 extraction types to the analysis model. The testimony, correspondence, and legislative records that represented most of Model A's advantage were sitting in the database, never delivered. When we fixed the pipeline to pass all extraction types, Model B's output improved dramatically — more than Model A's had originally.

The biggest improvement came not from switching extraction models but from fixing the pipeline to use data it already had. A code fix mattered more than a model upgrade.

Vision and text see different things

The Fort Berthold ledger — a dense tabular record of allottees, fee patent dates, and sale statuses — was extracted twice: once from OCR'd text, once by a vision model reading the page image directly. The text pipeline produced 311 records with pervasive column-misalignment errors: names from one row assigned to the allotment number of the next. In a sample of 18 disagreements, vision was correct 13 times (72%); text was correct 3 times (17%). All six Name disagreements went to vision.

But on handwritten affidavits, the result reversed. Vision missed 16 of 22 allotment numbers that text extraction captured, because the small typed margin headers were too faint for vision at 150 DPI. Each modality has blind spots.

OCR text extraction preserves margin annotations that vision misses. Vision preserves column alignment that OCR linearization destroys. For different document types in the same archive, you may need different extraction modalities.

Six Things I'd Tell a Colleague Starting This Work

  1. Fine-tuning doesn't transfer comprehension.

    Teaching a model the output format doesn't teach it to read carefully. The gap between models is reading skill, not JSON formatting.

  2. Match the model to the document type.

    One model found 59% more fee patents; the other found 80% more testimony. A multi-model pipeline outperforms any single model on a diverse archive.

  3. Fix the pipeline before switching models.

    The biggest quality improvement came from fixing a bug that discarded data before the analysis model could see it — not from a more expensive extraction model.

  4. The dangerous failure mode is confidence, not crashing.

    The AI never said "I don't know." It produced fluent, historically plausible text grounded in its training data instead of the archive. You have to verify the provenance of every claim.

  5. Budget 3–5× for infrastructure.

    GPU partition routing, format changes, memory errors, batch scheduler configuration — these consumed more hours than any research question. Plan for it.

  6. Check what the AI built, not just what it produced.

    When the same AI writes the pipeline and interprets the results, its blind spots compound. The developer and the analyst should be different people — or at minimum, you need to audit the code yourself.

The failure mode of LLM-assisted research isn't an error message. It's a confident, well-written answer that happens to be wrong.
