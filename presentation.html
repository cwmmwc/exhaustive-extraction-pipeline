Digital Humanities & AI

When the AI Is Confident
and Wrong

Lessons from using large language models to extract structured data from historical documents on Native American land dispossession

Christian McMillen · Department of History, University of Virginia · April 2026

A Finding

Pearly DeRoin, Iowa Allottee #35

In February 1940, the U.S. Attorney in Kansas filed a complaint on behalf of Pearly DeRoin, an Iowa allottee, against the Board of County Commissioners of Brown County. The county had been taxing her trust allotment—land the federal government held in trust and that was, by law, exempt from state and local taxation. By May, the county sent a check for $993.85: the full amount of the government’s claim, plus court costs.

Pearly DeRoin’s case is one found among 12,969 Department of Justice record slips from the National Archives. Her file number, 90-2-5-159, connects to six other slips tracing the arc of the case from filing through resolution. These slips are thin pieces of paper—typed or handwritten in the 1930s and 1940s—that I photographed and scanned at NARA. They sat in boxes for ninety years, unsearchable, until this project.

This is what the pipeline was built to find. Not one case—thousands of cases, across a corpus of nearly 5,000 federal documents documenting how Native Americans lost their land. Congressional hearings, BIA field reports, litigation files, correspondence, fee patent records—spanning 1880 to 1990, scattered across the National Archives and multiple university collections.

Data extraction means teaching AI to read these documents and pull out structured, searchable records. A 200-page congressional hearing is not searchable in any useful way as raw text. But if the AI can identify that on page 47, a witness named William Gates testified before Senator Wheeler on June 29, 1934, and claimed that 252 of 341 adult Yuma Indians supported the Wheeler-Howard bill—that becomes a record you can link to other records, count, map, and analyze across the full corpus.

The pipeline extracts ten types of structured data: named entities (people, organizations, places), events, financial transactions, fee patents, congressional testimony, correspondence, legislative actions, tax records, and mortgages. Of these, the fee patent—the instrument that converted trust land to alienable property—is the atomic unit of dispossession. Each fee patent record links an allottee to a parcel, an acreage, a date, a mechanism, a buyer, and often an attorney and a mortgage. Extracted at scale, these records reveal the machinery of land loss that no single document makes visible.

The infrastructure: commercial AI, open-source models, and UVA’s own GPUs

This pipeline doesn’t run on a single AI service. It uses a combination of three backends, chosen by task:

Claude Sonnet and Claude Opus (Anthropic) are commercial models accessed via API. Sonnet does the text extraction on narrative-heavy documents and all vision work (reading scanned index cards and tables). Opus does the analysis and synthesis—answering research questions against the extracted data. These are the most capable models we tested, and the most expensive: roughly $3–5 per document for extraction, $0.34 per analysis query.

Kimi K2.5 (Moonshot AI) is an open-source model that proved to be the best alternative to Claude for extraction. It reads the same documents and produces the same structured output, at 73% of Claude’s overall quality—but with a critical advantage: it found 59% more fee patents than Claude on allotment-heavy documents. Because it’s open-source, it can run on university-owned hardware at zero marginal cost.

UVA Research Computing GenAI (RC GenAI) is the key piece of infrastructure for this project. RC GenAI is a service operated by UVA Research Computing that provides access to large language models running on university-owned NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Kimi K2.5 is one of the models available through RC GenAI. For this project, it means the bulk extraction—reading thousands of pages of congressional hearings and producing structured records—runs on UVA hardware, at no cost to the researcher, with data staying on university systems. The 26 Survey of Conditions volumes extracted through RC GenAI cost $0 in API fees. Without RC GenAI, the same work through a commercial API would have cost approximately $55.

UVA HPC (Rivanna/Afton) provided additional GPU capacity for model benchmarking and for running the open-source vision model Qwen-VL on 87 index card PDFs. HPC jobs run on A100 80GB GPUs through the SLURM scheduler, independent of the researcher’s workstation.

Why local infrastructure matters

The combination of RC GenAI and HPC means that a UVA researcher can run state-of-the-art extraction models on sensitive archival materials without sending data to commercial cloud providers, and without per-document costs that make large-scale corpus work prohibitive. The commercial models (Claude) are reserved for tasks where they have a clear advantage—narrative analysis and vision extraction—while the volume work runs locally.

What It Found

Scale that no human reader could match

So far, the pipeline has extracted structured data from 780 documents totaling 53,000 pages across three archival collections: Crow Nation records, Kiowa/KCA records, and the Survey of Conditions of the Indians. The Survey of Conditions alone is 48 volumes of congressional hearings held between 1928 and 1943 across every Indian reservation in the country—23,565 pages of testimony, correspondence, and legislative debate. From that single collection, the pipeline yielded:

158,351 Structured Records
2,487 Fee Patents
5,492 Testimony Records

A separate vision extraction pipeline read 87 scanned PDFs of Department of Justice index cards and produced:

12,969 Record Slips
4,811 Legal Cases
6,588 Named Persons

An analysis layer uses a different AI model (Claude Opus) to synthesize across the extracted data—answering natural language questions against both the structured records and the full text. In a test on the 205-page hearing about the 18 unratified California treaties of 1851–52, the system surfaced details a human reader would need days to assemble: the $6M-to-$13M valuation dispute, a $49.5M direct appropriation bill that was never passed, enrollment rolls of 23,585 names, and the dying-witness problem that was eroding the evidentiary basis of the claims in real time.

A concrete research finding

The pipeline revealed that loan agents were negotiating mortgages on allotments before the fee patents were issued—meaning the apparatus of dispossession was in motion before the legal transfer of land even occurred. This pattern appears across multiple documents and collections, but is invisible to a researcher reading any single document in isolation. It required the structured extraction of thousands of individual fee patent records to see.

On aggregate, Claude Sonnet led the field. But on one document type—a 221-page report dense with individual allotment records—the open-source model Kimi K2.5 found 59% more fee patents than Claude:

Kimi K2.5268

“Which model is better?” turned out to be the wrong question. The right question is better at what, on which kind of document? Kimi excels on repetitive tabular records—the allotment-by-allotment details that are the core evidence of dispossession. Claude excels on narrative testimony and multi-party correspondence. The optimal pipeline uses both: Kimi for extraction (widest evidence net), Claude Opus for analysis (deepest interpretation).

Where It Went Wrong

The failures that shaped the pipeline

Every major improvement came from a failure that forced us to rethink an assumption. These are the instructive ones.

March 23–24
Fine-tuning made the model worse
We trained Llama 3.3 on 109 examples of Claude's extractions, expecting it to learn Claude's approach. Instead, performance dropped from 46% to 38% of Claude. 59% of training examples had empty fields—teaching the model that “correct” meant “sparse.” The gap between models isn’t format knowledge; it’s reading comprehension. You can’t transfer that in 109 examples. Cost: $71.
March 26–April
The AI assistant wired things wrong
Claude Code, the AI assistant building the pipeline, set up extractions to run through interactive sessions on the university’s high-performance computing cluster. This meant the extraction died whenever the researcher’s laptop closed or lost WiFi. The AI defaulted to interactive mode because it could see the output in real time. Its convenience drove an infrastructure choice that was wrong for the researcher.
April 2–9
Days of debugging for 40 lines of code
The university’s free GPU service started returning 99% failures. We filed support tickets. We reduced input sizes. We wrote retry logic. The actual problem: the service had silently switched its response format. Our parser was choking on the first six characters of every response. Days of debugging for a 40-line fix.
April 10
Eight women named Stella
We asked the system about tax records in a collection of 12,969 DOJ index cards naming 6,588 individuals. It returned an analysis dominated by eight women named Stella. The output was fluent, well-structured, and historically plausible. It was also wrong—the product of five stacked bugs. More on this below.
April 13
The code fix that mattered more than the model
A head-to-head comparison of two extraction models showed only modest differences. Investigation revealed the analysis layer was only sending 4 of 10 extraction types to the AI—the other 6 sat in the database, invisible. After the fix, re-running the same query with the cheaper model produced dramatically better results.

What the fixed pipeline produces

After all of these fixes, what does good output actually look like? Consider file number 90-2-5-118: U.S. v. Washabaugh County, et al.

The pipeline read 16 index card slips filed under this number and reconstructed the full arc of a tax recovery case for Hattie Hall, a Pine Ridge Sioux allottee. The county of Washabaugh, South Dakota, had been illegally taxing her trust allotment. The reconstruction traces:

March 1936 — Assistant U.S. Attorney files proposed complaint

April 1936 — Interior Department acknowledges the filing

May 1936 — Complaint filed in U.S. District Court, South Dakota

July 1936 — Affidavit of default—the county didn’t respond

February 1939 — $29.59 paid toward judgment

August 1939 — $24.47 paid toward judgment

April 1940 — $2.70 paid—then the payments stop

The county defaulted, lost, and then paid in dribbles—$29.59, $24.47, $2.70—before apparently abandoning the debt. Hattie Hall appears under 13 case name variants across the slips, all resolved by the pipeline to one canonical record. No single slip tells this story. The narrative only emerges when the pipeline links them together across the collection.

This is the kind of finding the pipeline produces at scale: not just data points, but case histories reconstructed from fragmentary bureaucratic records that were never designed to be read together.

The Core Risk

Silent failure in LLM-assisted research

The most dangerous failure wasn’t a crash or an error message. It was fluent, authoritative text that wasn’t grounded in the archive.

The Stella Bug

A query about tax records returned an analysis centered on eight women named Stella. The actual database contained 6,588 people—“Stella” appeared at a normal frequency of less than 1%. Five bugs stacked on top of each other:

1. Data retrieved, then thrown away. The code fetched tax and mortgage records from the database, then the prompt-building function silently dropped them. 1,553 tax records and 634 mortgage records were retrieved on every query and never seen by the AI.

2. No way to search the actual records. The search layer had no functions for the index card tables. Keyword search could only find records indirectly, through a handful of entity descriptions—which is where the Stella cluster came from.

3. The AI was told it had evidence it didn’t. The prompt said “YOU HAVE MULTIPLE TYPES OF EVIDENCE: testimony, taxes, mortgages…” regardless of what was actually provided. The AI was instructed to analyze data it couldn’t see.

4. When evidence was thin, the AI filled the gap from memory. With only a few entity matches to work with, the model substituted information from its training data. The output cited the Burke Act, the Dawes Act, and the Cato Sells competency commissions—all real, all historically accurate, and none of it from the researcher’s archive.

5. Natural language questions returned nothing. The search required every word to appear in a single database row. “Trace U.S. v. Pennington County” returned zero results because the word “trace” doesn’t appear in legal index cards. So the system fell back on its biased sample.

Why This Matters

The failure mode of an LLM-assisted research pipeline is not an error message. It is a confident, well-written, historically plausible answer that happens to be wrong. We caught it because “eight women named Stella” was absurd enough to notice. A subtler version—an analysis that draws 80% from the archive and 20% from the model’s training data—would be much harder to detect.

What We Learned

Six lessons from four weeks of iteration

1
The pipeline works—at a scale no human could match.
158,000 structured records from congressional hearings. 12,969 index cards read by machine vision. Research leads surfaced from 205-page documents in minutes, not days. The core promise of LLM-assisted archival research is real.
2
Different models are better at different things.
Kimi found 59% more fee patents than Claude on the same document. Claude found 80% more testimony items than Kimi. The optimal pipeline uses different models for different tasks—not the “best” model for everything.
3
The pipeline matters as much as the model.
Fixing the code to pass already-extracted data to the analysis model produced a bigger improvement than switching to a model that cost 10x more. The bottleneck was plumbing, not intelligence.
4
Fine-tuning doesn’t transfer comprehension.
Teaching a model the output format doesn’t teach it to read carefully. The gap between AI models on historical documents is reading comprehension, not JSON formatting. You can’t close it with 109 training examples.
5
LLM failure modes are silent.
The pipeline never crashed. It produced fluent, well-structured, historically plausible text that wasn’t grounded in the archive. In a research context, this is more dangerous than an error message. Domain expertise is the only reliable check.
6
AI assistants can introduce errors into their own analysis.
The AI assistant that built the pipeline also analyzed its output—and made incorrect claims about its own code. When one agent is both developer and analyst, you lose the natural check that comes from having different people in those roles.
